## 1. Executive Summary

> "We are called to be architects of the future, not its victims."
> — Buckminster Fuller

**Erdpuls is an open living lab protocol** — a replicable pattern for a Living Laboratory and Makerspace Garden where technology and nature collaborate to deepen our understanding of living systems, and where making, growing, observing, and repairing converge. It is a place to become human again — and, being a protocol rather than a place, it can take root anywhere a community chooses to grow it.

Each Erdpuls initiative unites two inseparable halves on a single site: a garden landscape and an adjacent making space. Together they form both the Living Laboratory and the Makerspace — not as separate functions in separate rooms, but as a unified hybrid in which every square metre serves science and making simultaneously. There is no line where the laboratory ends and the makerspace begins.

Erdpuls synthesizes three pedagogical streams — scientific rigour (the Head), practical design from living systems (the Hands), and contemplative perception (the Heart) — into a programme that closes the **Values–Action Gap**: the documented chasm where 84% of young Europeans value sustainability but only 30% translate that value into action. It is anchored in the Ubuntu philosophy ("I am because we are") and employs a four-element token economy on the Stellar network to structurally embed cooperation, reciprocity, mutualism, and regeneration into every interaction.

This white paper presents the philosophical foundations, the pedagogical architecture, the economic model, the digital systems, and the adoption pattern of the protocol — the reusable core that any bioregion, farm, school, or community can implement on its own ground. Its digital infrastructure and token economy are already live (see §11); the physical living lab described here is the model any community can now build.

## 2. The Problem: A Crisis of Perception

We live in an age of paradox. Never before have humans possessed such power to understand our planet. Never before have we been so disconnected from it. We can photograph galaxies thirteen billion light-years away, yet we cannot name the wildflowers in our own gardens. We carry supercomputers in our pockets, yet we have forgotten how to read the sky. We speak of "smart cities," yet our children believe food comes from supermarkets, not soil.

This is not merely an educational failure. This is a crisis of perception.

### 2.1 The Values–Action Gap

The evidence is stark: 84% of young Europeans say they value sustainability, yet only 30% translate this into sustainable behaviour. This Values–Action Gap — the chasm between knowing and doing, between intention and impact — represents the central challenge of environmental education in the 21st century.

We have educated minds that understand climate graphs but cannot grow a tomato. We have built systems that measure nature but refuse to participate in her rhythms. We have created technologies that extract facts from ecosystems but give nothing back. We have raised a generation that knows the price of everything but not the value of living systems.

### 2.2 The False Dichotomy

For too long, we have accepted a deceptive choice: Technology OR Nature. Facts OR Wisdom. Progress OR Preservation. This binary thinking has brought us to the edge. The dominant narrative presents technology and nature as fundamentally opposed — we must choose between preserving wilderness and advancing civilisation, between screen time and green time.

Erdpuls rejects this false dichotomy entirely. Technology is a human extension of natural creativity. When a spider spins a web or a bird builds a nest, we recognise these as natural behaviours employing sophisticated techniques. When humans develop tools and systems, we are doing the same thing. The question is never whether to use technology, but always how — toward what ends, guided by what values, with what consequences for the larger web of life.

### 2.3 The Disconnection

Children spend less time outdoors than any previous generation in human history, even as environmental challenges demand deeper ecological understanding. Adults have lost the rhythms of seasonal awareness. Education separates abstract knowledge from lived experience. Economic systems assign value to extraction and consumption while treating environmental stewardship as worthless unless it generates financial return. Technology increasingly mediates our experience of reality, even as we need unmediated sensory connection to the living world.

The edge, however, is also a threshold. And thresholds are where transformation begins.

## 3. Philosophical Foundations

### 3.1 Thinking Like a Plant

> "What if technology and nature are not opposites but complementary expressions of the same creative forces that shape our world?"
> — Erdpuls founding insight

At the philosophical heart of Erdpuls lies a radical proposition: that truly understanding living systems requires us to develop new modes of perception and cognition — what we call "thinking like a plant." Plants process information, respond to environment, communicate with neighbours, make decisions about resource allocation, remember past conditions, and anticipate future needs. They demonstrate intelligence of a fundamentally different kind than human cognition — distributed rather than centralised, temporal rather than immediate, relational rather than individualistic.

Seven plant wisdoms guide the work of Erdpuls:

**Rootedness:** A plant commits to place. It cannot flee from difficulty — it must transform it. In an age of endless mobility, we rediscover the power of staying, of belonging, of deep relationship with one place.

**Generosity:** A plant gives more than it takes. Oxygen, food, beauty, shelter — all offered freely. Our systems must learn this economy of abundance.

**Transformation:** A plant does not consume energy — it transforms it. Photosynthesis is alchemy. We seek not to consume the world but to participate in its transformation.

**Distributed Intelligence:** A plant has no central brain, yet it senses light, gravity, moisture, and chemical signals throughout its being. Wisdom need not be centralised to be powerful.

**Network Thinking:** Through mycorrhizal networks, plants share resources and warnings. The "Wood Wide Web" predates our internet by 450 million years.

**Rhythmic Living:** Plants live by rhythms — daily, seasonal, lunar. They do not fight time; they dance with it. We align our work with the pulse of the earth.

**Patient Accumulation:** A tree grows slowly, ring by ring, building something that can last centuries. No quarterly reports, no growth hacking — just steady becoming.

### 3.2 Ubuntu Philosophy: "I Am Because We Are"

The African philosophical tradition of Ubuntu recognises that individual identity and wellbeing emerge from community relationship and contribution. A person is a person through other people. This stands in profound contrast to hyper-individualism, where persons are imagined as autonomous individuals whose relationships are optional rather than constitutive.

In Erdpuls, Ubuntu principles operate at every level: individual learning happens through community participation; environmental health depends on collective care; economic value emerges from reciprocal contribution; knowledge builds cumulatively across generations; and resilience develops through interdependence. The **UBEC Protocol** — Ubuntu Bioregional Economic Commons — embeds these principles directly into its token mechanics.

### 3.3 Phenomenology and Direct Experience

Phenomenology insists that we begin with phenomena as they appear to direct experience rather than immediately jumping to theories. Rather than beginning with theory and looking for examples, Erdpuls begins with actual observation of actual plants growing in actual soil under actual weather conditions. Participants spend time simply being present with the garden, noticing what their senses perceive, before introducing names and categories.

Before checking what a temperature sensor reads, participants estimate temperature based on how it feels. Before consulting moisture readings, they feel the soil. This practice keeps the relationship right: technology extends and enhances human perception rather than replacing it. Participants develop both refined sensory awareness and technical facility, learning to trust their own direct experience while recognising that instruments can detect what human senses cannot perceive.

### 3.4 Reciprocity, Not Extraction

Most conventional environmental monitoring treats nature as an object from which data is extracted. The flow is fundamentally one-directional: from nature to human systems. Nature provides, humans take. Erdpuls proposes a different relationship based on reciprocity. We receive environmental data and ecological understanding from engagement with the garden. In return, we provide care, attention, and stewardship that maintains and enhances ecosystem health.

The token economy makes this reciprocity visible: participants earn tokens for environmental observations and garden care, recognising that their work creates genuine value. Observing, documenting, and caring for living systems should be recognised as valuable activities — not hobbies to be pursued only when independently wealthy. The reciprocal framing also shapes how we think about technology itself: sensors are not tools for extracting information from nature but ways of attending more carefully to what nature has to communicate.

### 3.5 Three Streams, One River

Erdpuls weaves three streams of wisdom into a single current:

- **KNOWING (Sustainability Science):** Rigorous understanding of complex systems, data literacy, the scientific method — the Head.
- **DESIGNING (Permaculture):** Practical patterns from living ecosystems, regenerative design, whole-systems thinking — the Hands.
- **ENSOULING (Contemplative Practice):** Deep perception, inner transformation, Goethean science — the Heart.

Head, hands, and heart — united. This is not education that merely informs. This is education that transforms.

## 4. The Model: A Living Laboratory and Makerspace Garden

### 4.1 What Is a Makerspace Garden?

A Makerspace Garden is neither a conventional makerspace with a garden attached, nor a garden with a workshop shed, nor a heritage renovation project with educational programming. It is a **unified infrastructure in which the same site is simultaneously a living laboratory for ecological observation and a makerspace for hands-on creation.**

Four principles define the model, and they hold regardless of where an initiative takes root:

**Growing, making, and building are continuous processes.** Plants grown in the garden become dye materials and biomaterial feedstock. Salvaged materials become garden infrastructure. Recycled plastic becomes new tools.

**Repair operates at every scale.** Fixing a neighbour's radio, tending a sick plant, and restoring an old wall all require the same ethic: diagnosis, patience, the right tools, and respect for the thing being cared for.

**Technology serves the garden and the buildings.** IoT sensors monitor both soil moisture in the growing beds and structural conditions in the buildings — extending human attention, not replacing it.

**Heritage is circular economy.** Restoring existing buildings with traditional techniques extends the life of materials that have already served for generations, turning conservation itself into curriculum.

### 4.2 The Unified Site

An Erdpuls site is organised as a single hybrid rather than a set of separate facilities. Three kinds of space interlock:

| Space | Character | Dual Function |
|---|---|---|
| **Garden landscape** | Outdoor, productive, contemplative | Living Lab: monitoring, citizen science · Makerspace: dye, fibre, seed, bio-materials, compost |
| **Building(s)** | Enclosed, climate-controlled where needed | Living Lab: building sensors, materials analysis · Makerspace: workshops, fabrication, repair, residency |
| **Transition zones** | Connecting, flowing shared edges | Outdoor classrooms, paths, tool storage, gathering fire — where observation and making meet |

The model uses a **gradient of enclosure** responsive to local climate: fully outdoor (garden, growing beds, fire pit), covered outdoor (courtyards, bicycle repair), semi-enclosed (buildings with open doors in warm seasons), and fully indoor (electronics lab, digital fabrication, seed storage, community kitchen). The specific mix — how many buildings, how large a garden, what climate strategy — is chosen by each initiative to fit its own place. What stays constant is that no square metre is single-purpose.

### 4.3 A Gradient of Ecosystems

The garden is designed to contain multiple distinct ecosystems, each chosen for ecological value and educational potential across all ages. A food forest designed according to permaculture principles mimics natural forest structure with multiple productive layers — canopy fruit and nut trees, understory shrubs, berry bushes, herbs, perennial vegetables, ground covers, root crops, and climbing vines. Annual vegetable gardens demonstrate different growing methods side by side: conventional row cropping, no-till methods, raised beds, and amended native soil. Wildflower meadows planted with native species create vital pollinator habitat. Wetland areas dramatically increase biodiversity. Composting and soil demonstration areas make visible processes that typically happen hidden. Indigenous-plant preservation zones honour both ecological and cultural values, featuring species native to the initiative's own bioregion.

At meaningful scale, the space becomes genuinely useful beyond education: food production provides real quantities of fresh produce, composting handles waste from multiple households, and the site becomes part of the community's operational infrastructure — not just an educational amenity.

## 5. The Five Functional Domains

Every Erdpuls initiative organises its making capacity into **five interconnected functional domains**. In a given implementation these may occupy separate buildings, shared rooms, or outdoor areas — the domains are defined by *function*, not by architecture, so a single-room initiative and a five-building campus can each realise all five. They are brought into service progressively, following the principle of Patient Accumulation: begin with whatever requires least investment to become useful.

### Domain A — Circular Economy Workshop

The public front door of the site. A regular **Repair-Café** creates the social foundation for everything else: residents bring broken electronics, textiles, bicycles, and furniture; volunteer repair coaches help fix them. A **Precious Plastic** station brings open-source plastic recycling to the community — collected waste is sorted, shredded, and transformed into markers, furniture components, and art objects. A textile repair corner connects to Domain B through natural dyeing. A community tool library, material exchange shelf, and bicycle repair station complete the ecosystem.

### Domain B — Bio-Materials Garden Laboratory

This domain spans indoors and outdoors — the garden is its primary territory, with covered space for seed storage, drying, and bio-material processing. A **dye garden** grows weld, indigo, madder, woad, marigold, onion skins, and walnut hulls. A **fibre garden** produces flax, hemp, nettle, and willow. Mushroom cultivation runs on outdoor logs and indoor substrate. A **community seed bank** preserves heirloom and locally adapted varieties as a biological commons. Bio-material experimentation — mycelium composites, bacterial cellulose, algae-based bioplastics — connects directly to Domain D's fabrication tools. Composting systems feed all material cycles.

### Domain C — IoT and Electronics Werkstatt

Where citizen science becomes hands-on. Arduino and Raspberry Pi workbenches, soldering stations, and electronics testing equipment enable participants to build their own environmental sensors. A LoRaWAN gateway provides distributed monitoring across the whole site. An e-waste refurbishment corner teaches materials literacy. A data-visualisation station displays real-time monitoring from garden and buildings. An open-source hardware library provides reference designs and documentation. A participant who builds a humidity sensor, deploys it in a wall, and uses the data to plan repairs has traversed the full arc from digital making to physical conservation.

### Domain D — Digital Fabrication Studio

Precision tools transform recycled plastic, site-grown biomaterials, local wood, and salvaged building materials into finished objects. 3D printers use filament extruded from Precious Plastic feedstock (Domain A). Laser-cut objects use biomaterial sheets grown on-site (Domain B). CNC-routed pieces use reclaimed wood from renovation salvage. Failed prints return to Domain A's shredder; sawdust becomes mushroom substrate in Domain B. Design workstations run open-source CAD. Custom replacement parts for household items, garden tools, and building hardware are produced on demand — all designs shared under open-source licences, in each initiative's working languages.

### Domain E — Heritage and Community Hub

The social heart. A community kitchen processes garden harvests, hosts cooking workshops, and prepares shared meals. A gathering space hosts workshops, lectures, seasonal celebrations, and exhibitions. A craft-demonstration area brings the conservation curriculum alive: visiting master craftspeople demonstrate traditional building and making techniques appropriate to the local heritage. A gathering fire becomes a focal point for solstice celebrations, harvest festivals, and the informal socialising that turns strangers into neighbours. A project-documentation library and OER archive preserve all knowledge generated on site.

### The Residency

Where an initiative has living space available, an **Artist and Researcher Residency** brings artists working with bio-materials and natural dyes, researchers conducting citizen science or conservation studies, visiting facilitators leading specialised workshops, and cross-cultural guests from partner organisations. Residencies are funded through the Collective Threshold Model and documented as OER.

## 6. The Seven Pillars

Erdpuls is structured around seven interconnected pillars — not separate programmes but facets of a single organism.

### 6.1 Sustainability Literacy (The Head)

Rigorous, data-driven understanding of complex ecological and material systems.

| Component | Description |
|---|---|
| IoT Sensor Networks | senseBox and compatible devices across the whole site: soil moisture, temperature, light, atmospheric pressure, structural humidity — coordinated through the IoT Service Hub. |
| Data Literacy | Participants learn to read, interpret, and generate environmental data via real-time dashboards, public APIs, and the data-visualisation station in Domain C. |
| Scientific Method | Citizen-science protocols ensure structured, repeatable observations. The **13 Questions to the Soil** module applies inquiry across sensory, ecological, and reflective dimensions. |
| Verifiable Data Integrity | Observations are recorded on the Stellar blockchain, creating permanent, tamper-evident environmental records. |
| Materials Science | Building conservation teaches clay composition, firing temperatures, mortar chemistry, and timber properties through direct contact with aged materials. |

### 6.2 Citizen Science (The Practice)

Democratising environmental and heritage research through community participation.

| Component | Description |
|---|---|
| Stewardship Network | Community members register as **stewards** through the Living Labs initiative, committing to regular environmental observation. |
| Phenomenological Observation | Goethean-science methods honour both human perception and technological sensing. Observers engage all senses before consulting instruments. |
| 13 Questions to the Soil | Structured community science: sensory (*How does the soil smell?*), visual (*What colour?*), tactile (*How does it feel?*), ecological (*What living beings?*), and reflective (*How does soil connect us?*) inquiry. |
| Hands-On Sensor Building | Participants build Arduino-based sensors in Domain C and deploy them across the site, learning electronics, programming, and data science. |
| Building Diagnostics | Sensors measuring structural humidity, temperature differentials, and micro-movement connect conservation directly to citizen science. |
| Open Science Data | All data transparent and accessible via public APIs. Map services provide geographic visualisation of bioregional data and community assets. |

### 6.3 Permaculture Design & Bio-Materials (The Hands)

Practical, regenerative design producing materials that feed the makerspace.

| Component | Description |
|---|---|
| Living Laboratory Site | A garden with permaculture zones, food forest, dye garden, fibre garden, mushroom cultivation, composting, and heritage plant beds. |
| Community Seed Bank | A seed library and exchange, collectively funded, preserving heirloom and locally adapted varieties as a biological commons. |
| Bio-Material Innovation | Mycelium composites, bacterial cellulose, algae-based bioplastics. Mycelium grows in 3D-printed moulds; bio-composite sheets are laser-cut into functional objects. |
| Workshop Programme | Introduction to Permaculture Design, Thinking Like a Plant, natural-dyeing intensives, fibre processing, and seasonal harvest programmes. |

### 6.4 Circular Economy & Repair (The Loop)

Closing material loops at every scale — from a broken radio to an old brick arch.

| Component | Description |
|---|---|
| Repair-Café | Regular community repair for electronics, textiles, bicycles, furniture. Free entry, funded through the Collective Threshold Model. |
| Precious Plastic Station | Open-source plastic recycling: shredder, injection moulder, extrusion machine, sheet press. Collected plastic becomes products and 3D-printer filament. |
| E-Waste Refurbishment | Old electronics assessed, repaired, and stripped for reusable components — teaching materials literacy. |
| Heritage Renovation | Restoring existing buildings with traditional techniques is circular economy enacted in brick and mortar, led by qualified craftspeople alongside community participants. |
| Material Flow Loops | Failed prints → shredder. Sawdust → mushroom substrate. Renovation salvage → garden edging. Dye plants → repaired textiles. Every waste stream becomes an input. |

### 6.5 Reciprocal Economics (The Exchange)

A regenerative economic model where value flows in multiple directions.

| Component | Description |
|---|---|
| Four-Element Token Ecosystem | **UBEC** (Air / Diversity): gateway and universal access. **UBECrc** (Water / Reciprocity): flow and exchange. **UBECgpi** (Earth / Mutualism): stable relationships. **UBECtt** (Fire / Regeneration): catalytic transformation. All on the Stellar network. (See §7.) |
| Collective Threshold Model | Offerings publish costs transparently and proceed only when the community collectively funds them. Privacy-protected contributions prevent stigma. |
| Four Participation Pathways | Full Rate (sustaining), Supported Rate (pay what fits), Skills Exchange (e.g. a bricklayer teaches pointing in return for electronics training), and UBECrc Tokens (citizen science earns makerspace participation). |
| Holonic Evaluation | Five-dimensional assessment: Autonomy–Integration, Ubuntu Alignment, Reciprocity Health, Mutualism Capacity, Regeneration Impact. |

### 6.6 Heritage Conservation & Place Identity (The Roots)

Rootedness in place, time, and cultural heritage.

| Component | Description |
|---|---|
| Vernacular Architecture | An initiative's own building heritage — its local materials, bonding patterns, doorways, and decorative craft — becomes both subject and classroom. |
| Renovation as Curriculum | Each renovation teaches materials science, structural engineering, circular economy, heritage craftsmanship, project management, and IoT building monitoring. |
| Heritage Craft Transmission | Traditional building and making skills — disappearing from many rural regions — are transmitted intergenerationally. |
| Conservation-Authority Integration | Connection to regional heritage-conservation bodies, potential conservation funding, and the site's use as a training ground for heritage building skills. |

### 6.7 Contemplative Practice & Ecological Consciousness (The Heart)

Inner transformation and deep perception as the foundation for authentic engagement.

| Component | Description |
|---|---|
| Goethean Science | Phenomenological observation beginning with direct sensory engagement — perceiving natural processes as they unfold rather than reducing them to abstract data. |
| The 4A Pathway | Awareness (sensing) → Acknowledgement (personal responsibility) → Attitude (internalising values) → Action (measurable stewardship, immediately recognised through tokens). |
| Anthroposophical Pedagogy | A deliberate symbiosis of anthroposophical pedagogy with contemporary sensing and token technology — education that transforms, not merely informs. |
| Seasonal Rhythms | Spring: planting and planning. Summer: growing and gathering. Autumn: processing and preserving. Winter: repairing and reflecting. |

## 7. The Token Economy: Four Elements of Cooperation

The UBEC Protocol embodies Ubuntu wisdom in a token-based economic system where cooperation, reciprocity, and regeneration are structurally embedded in the mechanics. Four tokens map to the four classical elements, each representing a fundamental principle:

| Element | Token | Principle | Function |
|---|---|---|---|
| Air | **UBEC** | Diversity | Gateway and universal access. Like air, freely available to all, supporting diverse participation. |
| Water | **UBECrc** | Reciprocity | Flow and exchange. Like water through a watershed, facilitating balanced giving and receiving. |
| Earth | **UBECgpi** | Mutualism | Stability and value. Like earth providing foundation, enabling mutually beneficial relationships. |
| Fire | **UBECtt** | Regeneration | Transformation and action. Like fire catalysing change, enabling regenerative transformation. |

*(Live supply and circulation figures for each token are published on the Bioregional Network dashboard rather than fixed in this document — the protocol defines the roles; the network reports the numbers.)*

The reciprocal loop is three-dimensional: citizen science generates tokens, tokens fund makerspace workshops, workshops build sensors, better sensors enable better citizen science. Heritage renovation builds community skills, skills expand community capacity, capacity enables further renovation. Garden cultivation produces materials, materials become products, products generate income, income supports more cultivation.

The **Collective Threshold Model** governs all offerings: costs are published transparently, participants register interest, and the community collectively funds each offering through privacy-protected contributions. Four participation pathways ensure no one is excluded: Full Rate, Supported Rate, Skills Exchange, and UBECrc Tokens.

## 8. Bridging the Values–Action Gap: The 4A Pathway

How does Erdpuls close the gap between the 84% who value sustainability and the 30% who act? Through a structured pathway that applies equally to garden observation and to making practice:

| Stage | In the Garden | In the Makerspace |
|---|---|---|
| **Awareness** | Observing environmental data, natural phenomena, seasonal rhythms, soil life | Understanding materials: where they come from, what they cost the earth, where they go when discarded |
| **Acknowledgement** | Recognising personal connection to ecological systems and the land beneath our feet | Recognising that our buildings, tools, and objects carry environmental and cultural stories |
| **Attitude** | Internalising sustainability values through sustained, patient observation | Internalising circular thinking: "Can this be repaired? Reused? Returned to the soil?" |
| **Action** | Contributing citizen-science data, growing materials, tending the seed library, earning UBECrc tokens | Repairing objects, building sensors, fabricating with circular materials, renovating buildings |

The gap closes when action becomes immediate, tangible, and recognised. A child who checks soil moisture each morning — not because a teacher requires it, but because she cares for plants she knows by name — has closed the gap. A community member who repairs a neighbour's radio rather than watching it be discarded has closed the gap. The pathway works because it is embedded in daily practice, not confined to a curriculum.

## 10. Adopting the Protocol: An Implementation Pattern

Erdpuls is designed to be adopted, not franchised. An initiative does not need five buildings, a specific budget, or a particular location — it needs a garden, a making space, a community, and a commitment to the principles. The pattern below describes *how* an initiative comes into being; the *scale* is chosen locally.

The sequence follows the principle of **Patient Accumulation**: bring capacity into service progressively, beginning with whatever requires least investment to become genuinely useful.

**Phase 1 — Germinate.** Activate the site with minimal renovation and maximum community engagement. Open the Circular Economy Workshop (Domain A) first — a Repair-Café needs little more than tables, tools, and willing hands, and it builds the social foundation for everything else. Establish the first garden beds and a seed library. Stand up basic environmental sensing (Domain C) so citizen science can begin. Establish the initiative's identity and join the network directory.

**Phase 2 — Grow.** Systematically expand: bring the Bio-Materials Laboratory (Domain B) and Digital Fabrication Studio (Domain D) into service, extend the sensor network across the whole site, begin bio-material experiments, and build partnerships — regional schools, conservation bodies, maker networks, and the open-source communities (Precious Plastic, open hardware) whose work the protocol builds on.

**Phase 3 — Mature and Radiate.** Reach full capacity across all five domains, pursue energy self-sufficiency, publish the initiative's research and conservation documentation as OER, and become a training site that helps *other* communities adopt the protocol. Each mature initiative lowers the threshold for the next.

Because the buildings and land — once cared for — become permanent community assets rather than leased premises, the model compares favourably over time to the recurring cost of conventional urban makerspace or education premises. What an initiative invests, it keeps.

## 11. Status: What Is Running Today

The protocol is not theoretical. Its **digital infrastructure and token economy are already operational**, independent of any single physical site:

- The **four-element token economy** — UBEC, UBECrc, UBECgpi, UBECtt — is live on the Stellar network, with supply and circulation reported on the Bioregional Network dashboard.
- The **senseBox / IoT citizen-science layer** reports environmental readings through the IoT Service Hub, alongside the **13 Questions to the Soil** module.
- The **Collective Threshold Model** runs on the Erdpuls Portal, and a **community seed bank** has been funded through collective, privacy-protected contribution.
- The four digital hubs (§9) are deployed and multilingual (English, German, Polish, Ukrainian), with all platform code open-source and documentation under CC BY-SA 4.0.

What does **not** yet exist is a full **physical** Erdpuls campus — the unified garden-and-buildings site with its five functional domains realised in brick and soil. That is precisely what the protocol now invites communities to build. The digital commons is running and ready; the first physical living lab awaits the ground, the community, and the hands to grow it.

## 12. Conclusion: Growing the New World

Erdpuls is not a conventional environmental education centre, nor a conventional makerspace, nor a conventional research lab. It is all three at once — a single organism woven together by a regenerative philosophy that refuses the false choice between technology and nature, between heritage and innovation, between making and growing.

As a **Living Lab**, it transforms a bioregion into a permanent, sensor-rich, community-governed research environment where every participant is simultaneously a learner, a scientist, and a steward — and where the buildings themselves become research subjects, monitored by the very sensors built in the on-site workshops.

As a **Makerspace Garden**, it expands the definition of "making" to include making knowledge (through citizen science), making soil (through permaculture), making data (through IoT), making community (through reciprocal economics), making consciousness (through contemplative practice), and making heritage endure (through conservation as curriculum). The garden feeds the workshops; the workshops create tools that enhance observation; the buildings' renovation generates knowledge; and the community that gathers in both spaces provides the social energy that sustains everything.

Its seven pillars — Sustainability Literacy, Citizen Science, Permaculture Design, Circular Economy, Reciprocal Economics, Heritage Conservation, and Contemplative Practice — are not separate programmes but facets of a single organism. Five functional domains organise the making. Four digital hubs connect everything. And a single question animates it all: *What if we learned to think like a plant?*

Because Erdpuls is a protocol rather than a place, it travels. Each initiative that takes root — in its own soil, its own architecture, its own languages — continues an old tradition: a meeting point between technology and nature, between heritage and innovation, between making and growing, between the hand that trowels mortar and the hand that solders a circuit board. A place to become human again.

> "You never change things by fighting the existing reality. To change something, build a new model that makes the existing model obsolete."
> — Buckminster Fuller

We are not fighting the old world. We are growing the new one. We are making. We are repairing.
